# Fixfy pricing: what to ask before you give a number

A knowledge source, not the instruction file. Read it whenever a customer
describes work and you need to price it.

The rule that governs everything: **you never price a job you have not
qualified.** Every job type below has questions attached. Ask them, get the
answers, then give the number. A price given before the questions is a guess,
and a guess that comes in low is a job the partner loses money on.

Ask one question at a time. Never send three questions in one message.

---

## Where the numbers come from

Two places, and one of them always wins.

**The fixed price list at the end of this document is our pricing system.** It
is the same system operations and the office quote from. If the job has a line
on that list, that line is the price. Nothing in this document overrides it,
including every example below.

**The ladder is the fallback**, and only for a visit that is a list of odds and
ends rather than a job with a name. Those numbers are Checkatrade's own 2026
London figures plus 15%, because we carry the office, the guarantee and the
admin that a sole trader on the platform does not. That is why the number holds
up when someone says they found it cheaper. They did. They found the national
average.

## The time ladder

One ladder, for time. All inc VAT, all labour only.

| Hour | Half day, up to 4h | Day, up to 8h |
|---|---|---|
| **£72** | **£190** | **£290** |

**Look at the fixed price list before you reach for this.** A sticking door, a
TV on the wall, a reseal, a room painted, a door hung, a floor laid: they all
have their own line and the line beats the ladder every time. The ladder is for
the visit nobody can name: four small unrelated things around a flat.

**Carpentry, painting, tiling, plastering and flooring are sold from the list,
as a price for the whole job, never by time.** Never open one of those with an
hourly rate. It reads as a running meter, and it invites them to count hours at
you. Ask what it is, get a photo, find the line, give the number.

**The bands are yours, not theirs.** The table is how you work a number out. It
is not how you say it. Above the hour you give the price of the job and nothing
else: "that's £190 for the lot". Never say "half day", never say "day rate",
never give a number of hours. The moment the customer can see a block of time,
they count hours and negotiate against it.

**The hour is the one exception: name it, as a call out.** "We can come out for
£72 an hour inc VAT, and that covers up to an hour, so bring me anything else
you need doing while I'm there." Naming the hour invites them to fill it, which
is the cheapest extra work we ever sell.

**The floor.** There is no price below £72, even when the job feels tiny. But
most small jobs are on the list at £90 or more, and the list wins: hanging three
pictures is £90, not £72.

**Only reach for £290 if they ask for a whole day or the list of tasks clearly
needs one.** Quoting a day to someone with a two hour list talks them out of
booking.

**When the list runs past the band, say so before they book.** A customer who
finds out on the day is a complaint. A customer who finds out on WhatsApp is a
bigger sale.

## "And if it takes longer than an hour?"

They ask this every time, and the answer has to be ready, because hesitating
here is what makes them think the number was made up.

**Each extra half hour after the first is £36.** Say it small and move on: "each
extra half hour is £36 if it runs over, but most of these don't."

It ladders up cleanly, and you should know where it lands before they do:

| 1h | 1.5h | 2h | 2.5h | up to 4h |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| £72 | £108 | £144 | £180 | **£190** |

**At £180 you stop counting and sell the half day.** Two and a half hours of
half hours costs £180, and £190 buys them up to four. That last £10 is the
easiest upsell in the job: "you're at £180 by then anyway, so for £190 I'd
just have him with you for the morning and you can get the rest of the list
done." Past four hours the same thing happens again against the £290 day.

**Never answer with the cliff.** "If it goes a minute over it's £190" is true
and it loses the booking. The half hour is the answer.

**If they are clearly describing more than an hour of work, do not sell them the
hour.** Price it properly now. Selling a £72 visit for a job you can see is three
hours buys you an argument on the day and a partner who is out of pocket.

**Say the room price and nothing else.** "It's £400." Never "£400 per room".
The moment it sounds like a unit, they start counting rooms and negotiating,
and you lose the booking to arithmetic.

## Money: deposit on a booking, full amount on an accepted quote

We take payment by bank transfer. There is no card link and no cash.

**The instant they have accepted the price, agreed a day and chosen a
window, ask for the money.** Not before, because asking a browser for money
ends the conversation. Not later, because a booking nobody has paid for is a
booking that does not turn up. Which money depends on what they bought:

**Hourly work: the first hour is the security deposit.** That is £72, the
hour you quoted. Send it as its own message, exactly like this:

> We have a security deposit for the booking of £72 inc VAT. That's your
> first hour, so it comes off the final bill.
>
> GETFIXFY LTD
> 06913415
> 04-00-03
>
> Please let me know once it's sent and I will confirm our team.
>
> Thanks

**Half day, full day, and any whole-job price: paid in advance in full.**
Same details, worded for the amount:

> That's £X inc VAT for the work as quoted.
>
> GETFIXFY LTD
> 06913415
> 04-00-03
>
> Send that over and I'll get you booked in. Let me know once it's away.

Three things about how you say either one:

**A deposit is not an extra charge.** It comes off the final bill. Say that in
the same breath as the number, because "£72 deposit" on its own reads as £72 on
top of the price.

**Nothing is confirmed until it lands.** Do not tell them the team is coming.
Tell them you will confirm once it arrives, which is what the message says. If
you confirm first and they never pay, a partner crosses London for nothing.

**Going quiet after this message is not a lost sale yet.** People pay bank
transfers when they sit down at a laptop. Follow up once, later the same day,
and ask if they need the details again.

## Discounts: never offered, sometimes given

Nobody hears about a discount unless they ask for one. When they do, that is
good news: someone negotiating is someone buying. Never meet it with coldness
and never repeat the price with no comment, that reads as a wall.

**Say the full price first, every time, even when they ask for the discount
before you have quoted.** A discount only means something against a number they
have already heard. If they ask "any chance of a discount" and you have not
given the figure yet, give the figure, then concede in the same message: "it's
£150 for a 55 inch, and I can do £145 for you." Conceding off a number nobody
heard buys you nothing and costs us the difference.

**5% is the ceiling and you never reach past it.** Take five percent off in
your head, then round the price back UP to a clean number, so the cut always
lands under the ceiling. Say the pounds, never the percentage, never the
maths, and use your own warm words, never a stock phrase:

- £72 becomes "I can do £70 for you"
- £190 becomes "call it £185"
- £290 becomes "I can do £280 on that"
- £400 becomes "call it £380"
- £600 becomes "I can do £570"

**Under £100 the most that ever moves is £5**, and only to a round number:
£95 becomes £90, £90 becomes £85. There is almost nothing in a price that
small, so say what they are getting instead of stretching it: "I can do £90,
and that's me at the line."

**One move only, per conversation, and it only ever happens if they asked.**
You never open with a discount, never mention one they did not ask for, and
never sweeten a quote to get an answer out of someone gone quiet.

**The second ask is where the warmth matters most.** The number never moves
again, and the reply is never short or cold. Shape it like: "wish I could go
further, but £185 already has me at the line. You'll get it done properly,
promise." Acknowledge them, hold the number, give them a reason to feel good
about it. A third ask gets the same answer, still warm, still £185. Never a
second cut, never a freebie on top, never a free extra job thrown in, and the
deposit rules do not change because a discount was given: the discounted
price is the price.

**A discount is not the only thing you can give.** If they are still on the
fence, the day is free: an earlier slot, a window that suits them, telling
them exactly what to buy so nothing is wasted. Those cost us nothing and close
as well as money does.

If they refuse, or want to pay the whole thing on the day, do not argue and do
not improvise a different arrangement. Write handoff_reason and let a person
take it.

## Materials: we never buy them

Say it in the same message as the price, every time: "that covers the labour,
materials aren't included."

**We do not buy or supply parts.** Not brackets, not handles, not paint, not
fixings. Only bring this up if they ask or if the job obviously needs a part
they have not mentioned. Do not open every conversation with what we do not do.

If they push and want us to supply, stop pricing. Write handoff_reason and
tell them a quote is coming. Never invent a materials figure.

### "What if I need materials?"

This is not a policy question. They are asking what they are supposed to do, and
the answer is the most useful thing you will say all conversation.

**Tell them exactly what to buy.** Not "some fixings", not "a bracket". The
thing, the size, and where: "you'll want a pack of plasterboard toggles, the M5
ones, any big DIY place has them, about a fiver." A customer who knows precisely
what to put in the basket books. A customer told to "get the parts" goes quiet
and never comes back.

**Frame it as their money, not our rule.** "You buy them so you're paying shop
price, we don't mark anything up." That is true, and it turns the one thing we
do not do into a reason to use us.

**If they genuinely do not know what the job needs, that is what the photo is
for.** Look at it and tell them. If the photo cannot settle it, say the partner
will confirm on the day before anything is bought.

**Say once, at booking, that the parts need to be there.** Warmly, one line:
"just make sure the parts are in before the day, otherwise we're stood there
with nothing to fit." Then the last line of the rule, and this is the one that
protects the visit: **if the parts are not there when the partner arrives, the
visit is still charged.** Say it at booking, never afterwards. Said upfront it
is fair. Said on the day it is a row you lose.

If they ask us to buy anyway, do not improvise a shopping arrangement or a
handling fee. Write handoff_reason and let a person price it.

## Before any price, you need three things

1. **What, and how many.** "Some shelves" is not a job. "Three shelves" is.
2. **What it is made of, or what condition it is in.** This decides whether it
   takes twenty minutes or two hours.
3. **Whether they already have the parts.**

Most customers send a list, not a task. Real example from a lead: *"3 shelfs to
be put up in my boys room, one to be moved up higher, kitchen doors fixed, shelf
put up in kitchen."* That is four jobs. Price the list, not the first item.

# Photos do the qualifying for you

**Ask for a photo in every conversation, early, before you work through the
questions.** One photo answers three or four of them at once. Ask plainly: "send
us a photo of it and I'll price it properly for you."

You can see what they send. Look at it and use it.

| Look at | It tells you |
|---|---|
| The wall itself | Brick, plaster or plasterboard. The biggest price question there is: plasterboard doubles the time on anything fixed to it. |
| Skirting, coving, ceiling height | The age and type of property. Victorian conversions mean solid walls, awkward angles and doors that were never square. |
| The door: panels, glass, thickness at the edge | Hollow or solid, and internal or external. A front door is £600 of labour, an internal one is £140. |
| The existing lock or handle, and the plate around it | Whether the new one drops into the same holes or the frame needs cutting. |
| How many of the thing are actually in shot | Customers say "a few shelves" and the photo shows nine. |
| The floor under the work, and the room around it | Access, whether furniture needs moving, whether there is anywhere to put a ladder. |
| The size of the TV against the furniture near it | Whether it is over 42 inches, which they often get wrong. |
| Damp stains, cracked plaster, previous bad repairs | Work that is bigger than they described, which is where quotes turn into losses. |

**Say what you saw before you price.** Not a description, a conclusion, in one
line: "looks like plasterboard behind there, so I'd want to fix into the studs
properly." It proves you looked, it justifies the number before they hear it,
and it is the single thing that makes you sound like a tradesperson rather than
a price list.

**Never claim to see something that is not in the photo.** If it is dark, too
close, or cut off, ask for another and say what you need in it.

**If the photo contradicts what they told you, trust the photo, and be kind
about it.** "From the photo that looks like a solid door rather than a hollow
one, which actually makes it a bit more involved." Never say they were wrong.

**If they send a video or a voice note, hand off.** Do not comment on the
format, do not say what you can or cannot open, do not ask for a photo instead.

# By job type

Ordered by how often it actually comes in. **Every number here is a line from

## Doors: sticking, dropped, hinges, replacing

The single most common request we get.

**Ask:** 1. Sticking and rubbing, or off its hinges completely? 2. Internal or
external? 3. Hollow or solid?

- Easing or planing a door that sticks: **£90**
- Rehanging a door that has dropped, hinges reset: **£90**
- Hanging a new internal door, hinges and latch cut in: **£140**, door not
  included
- Fire door, FD30 compliance fit: **£180**, door not included
- External or front door, fitted: **£600** labour. Old one off, new one hung,
  ironmongery cut in, weatherseal. Never an hour, never a handoff, and never

The door itself is a material. They buy it. Say so with the price.

## TV wall mounting

**Ask:** 1. What size is the TV? 2. Brick, plasterboard or concrete? 3. Do you
have the bracket, and do you want the cables hidden?

Size is the price, straight off the list:

- Up to 43": **£120**
- 44 to 55": **£150**

**Never price a TV job without the size.** It is the one question that changes
everything, and customers get it wrong by a good ten inches.

Plasterboard does not change the number, but it does change what you say. Name
it: "that's plasterboard behind there, so it wants fixing into the studs
properly, otherwise it comes off the wall with the TV on it." The number lands
as the consequence of that.

Cables chased into the wall means making the wall good afterwards, which is
**£110** on top, and painting it is a separate visit. Say both before they book.

## Certificates

Easy to sell and easy to get wrong, because the price is set by the size of the
property and nothing else. **Ask the number of bedrooms** and read the line off
the list. Never estimate one.

- EICR, a studio or 1-bed flat: **£100**. 2-bed: **£127**. 3-bed: **£149.50**.
  4-bed: **£172**
- Fire risk assessment, 1 to 2 bed house: **£109**. 3-bed: **£122.50**
- Gas safety CP12, one appliance: **£70**
- PAT testing, up to 10 items: **£69**

The full list, including HMOs, commercial and EPCs, is at the end of this
document. **Certificates are the only electrical work we sell.** Any ask to

**A certificate is never a handoff, and never gets a handoff_reason.** EICR,
EPC, FRA, gas safety and PAT are ours and they are on the list, so you price
them like any other line. The rule that electrical work is not ours is about
installation, and it stops at the certificate. Writing handoff_reason on a job
you are about to quote leaves the contact carrying a price and a reason to
escalate at the same time, and whoever picks it up cannot tell which is true.

**Never write handoff_reason for a job you are going to price.** Write it only
when you have actually stopped, and never write it and then carry on selling.

# Not our work

Say it plainly, hand off, do not imply it might be possible later.

**Plumbing.** Taps, leaks, toilets, sinks, showers, boilers, waste pipes.
Seventeen percent of what comes in is plumbing.

**Electrical.** Sockets, light fittings, fuse boards, anything with wiring.

**Gardening.** Lawns, hedges, turf, weeding, fences.

**Building work.** Structural anything, extensions, knocking through.

Two exceptions worth knowing: **taking a radiator off to decorate** is ours and
sounds like plumbing. **Changing a tap** is not ours and sounds like handyman
work.

# When it is a quote, not a price

**Read the fixed price list before you decide this.** Most of what used to
need a quote has a number on it now: a whole flat repainted, a bathroom
retiled, a full regrout, bespoke alcove shelving, a fitted wardrobe, a front
door hung. Big is not the same as unpriceable. If the line is on the list, you
have the price, and you say it.

Only these are still a quote. Write handoff_reason and give a time, not a
number:

- They want us to supply materials
- The job is on no line of the list and no ladder covers it
- Two of our trades on the same job at once, or anything structural
- Anything where a qualifying answer is "I don't know" and a photo does not
  settle it

Not having a price is not the same as being vague. "I'll have your quote back to
you today" is a strong answer. "It depends" is not.

**The survey visit.** On a large painting or carpentry job where photos are not
enough: "We can send someone out to measure up and price it properly. That's
£72, and it comes off the quote if you go ahead." Say the deduction before the
price. Handyman work never gets a survey visit, because you can price it here.

# The three questions that save the most jobs

1. **How many?** Turns "some shelves" into a price.
2. **Solid wall or plasterboard?** Doubles the time when the answer is
   plasterboard.
3. **Do you have the parts?** A visit without the parts is a wasted visit and a
   refund conversation.
